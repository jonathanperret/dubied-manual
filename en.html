INTRODUCTION.

These Instructions might equally be titled An Introduction to Machine Knitting on the Dubied Hand Machine. They were written to acquaint the beginner gradually and methodically with her machine. The order of the subjects follows the teaching given in our knitting schools, so that the beginner may study our instructions paragraph by paragraph, never turning a page before she has fully understood it and carried out on her machine the instructions it gives.

As soon as she has grasped the general working of her machine and knows a first kind of stitch, the knitter puts her knowledge into practice by making a simple article: a scarf. She then learns other kinds of stitch, which allow her to knit in turn a pullover, a skirt and a sleeved jacket. Next come fancy stitches and various kinds of pattern, which are used to make a cardigan. Finally the knitter completes her training by making stockings and socks, which already call for a certain skill.

The knitter then knows enough to fend for herself and to reproduce with ease the varied articles that appear each month in the Revue du Tricotage published by us, or to create new articles as her fancy takes her.

The information given relates to the DUBIED machine of type MM, 60 cm. wide and of gauge 32 or 36. Those who own a Dubied hand machine of another type, M, MR or CS, can nevertheless use these instructions in full, for all our hand machines work on the same principle. On pages 59 and 60 we give some details relating to the M, MR and CS machines, and we ask those who own one of these machines to kindly refer to those pages during their training.

Main parts of the knitting machine.

The flat knitting machine with latch needles was built for the first time in Europe, at Couvet (Switzerland) in 1867, by Edouard Dubied senior. The modern machine of the Dubied firm consists of a roof-shaped cast-iron frame that supports the two needle beds, also simply called beds. These are two steel plates milled with grooves in which the needles slide.

Above the needle beds runs the carriage, guided in its to-and-fro movement by slide bars. The carriage bears mechanisms called cams or locks, which move the needles. At each passage of the carriage along the needle beds the needles rise and fall, and each forms a stitch.

The needles (see a, fig. 3) end at one end in a swelling or butt d, on which the cam boxes act; at the other end in a hook b and a hinged tongue or latch c, which serves to form the stitch.

Latch needles work in the same way as ordinary crochet hooks: with the latch open, that is, folded back on the body of the needle, a yarn is caught with the hook and the needle is moved forward so that this yarn slides behind the latch; a new yarn is caught in the hook as the needle is drawn back, the first yarn closes the latch, slides over it and over the hook, and drops astride the strand of the 2nd yarn held by the hook, thus forming a stitch (see fig. 3).

In the Dubied knitting machine the needles are arranged side by side and form stitches in succession from the same yarn; this yarn travels along the row of needles alternately from right to left and from left to right. In this way one obtains a strip of knitting whose width depends on the number of needles used. The fineness of the knitting depends mainly on the spacing of the needles.

The carriage.

The carriage is driven by a crank to which it is joined by a connecting rod. This crank can be shortened, that is, it can be slid from left to right in the crank head, after loosening the screw o, fig. 1, with a key and pulling out the small stop plunger next to the screw head.

It is advisable to shorten the crank when knitting narrow articles that need only a limited field of needles. With the shortened crank one will work faster and more easily.

The carriage consists of a bridge extended on each side by two plates containing the cam box mechanisms and the cams. The carriage is driven by means of the crank. When the carriage is stopped at one end of the machine, it tends to run back, drawn by the weight of the crank; to avoid this drawback, stops have been placed at both ends of the travel.

To stop the carriage on the left, engage on the connecting rod the hook (fig. 4) located beside the crank handle; to stop it on the right, engage under the connecting rod the small plunger (fig. 5) that passes through the crank head.

The guiding of the carriage by the slide bars is extremely precise thanks to an extension C (fig. 2), which bears on the slide through an adjustable gib screwed to the left end of the extension.

The cam box.

The cam box is the name given to the cams and their support as a whole.

The raising cams A (fig. 6) make the needles rise. They are put in work by pulling the cam-control pins 1, 2, 3 and 4 (fig. 2) towards the outside of the carriage, and out of work by pushing the pins inwards.

7

The stitch cams C (fig. 6) make the needles descend and determine the length of the stitches. The setting of each stitch cam is shown by the graduated scale (fig. 7), along which a pointer c moves. On the rod bearing the pointer is a stitch-cam knob a, which serves to fix the stitch cam.

The operation of placing the stitch cams at a given position is called “setting the knobs”. This setting is given, for the various kinds of stitch,

on a special sheet supplied with the machine, called the “Knob Setting Sheet”.

To avoid breaking stitches when using fragile yarns, one can work with free stitch cams (free knobs), that is, set the position of the knobs without tightening them.

For this the knob stops are used, as follows (fig. 7):

bring the pointer c to the desired position, tighten the knob a, loosen the screw d a little, press the stop e against the pointer c, and firmly retighten the screw d;

unscrew the knob 2 or 3 turns, screw in the lock screw b by hand, then screw the knob back to hold it on the rod of the stitch cam. The stitch cam will then be free. To fix the stitch cams again, release the knob, unscrew the screw b by hand, then screw down the knob.

It is not essential to lock the knobs if only a few rows are knitted with the stitch cams free.

The brushes.

The brushes fixed to the carriage open the needle latches, so that the needle hooks are sure to take the yarn. They are placed so as to skim the needles, with the bevel facing upwards.

Diagram A (fig. 8) shows a correct position of the brushes, diagram B a faulty position, which causes rapid wear of the brushes.

The yarn changer (see fig. 2).

The yarn changer is the mechanism used to bring the yarn to the needles. Its essential parts are the yarn carriers F and B, which slide one on the front of the machine, the other on the rear.

The yarn carriers are driven by the carriage by means of the plungers A and E. These are controlled by the lever D. Pushing this lever to the left puts the rear yarn carrier in work and the front yarn carrier out of work. Pushing it to the right gives the opposite result.

The change of yarn carrier is always made with the carriage to the right of the machine.

The yarn-carrier tip B (fig. 9) is the very hard tempered steel piece at the end of the yarn carrier A. It is pierced with a hole through which the yarn passes. So that the yarn runs very smoothly, the parts of the yarn-carrier tip it touches are carefully polished and rounded.

The counter.

A small counter, reading up to 1000 cycles and driven by the crank shaft, shows the number of cycles of the carriage. To make good use of the counter, one need only set it to zero when beginning work.

Preparatory work.

Unpacking and installing a machine.

Take off the lid, remove the accessories and the various parts fixed to the inner walls of the crate, then unscrew from the outside the wooden crosspieces that wedge the machine.

Stand the crate on two chairs and unscrew from underneath (from the outside) the 4 screws holding the machine to the bottom of the crate.

Finally lift out the machine, holding it by the bars near the point where they meet the frame, and not by the ends of the bars, which the weight of the machine could bend.

This unpacking must in all cases be done easily and without forcing anything, so as not to risk damaging the machine.

Place the machine on the cast-iron stand (N, fig. 1) and fix it with the same 4 screws that held it to the bottom of the crate.

If the cast-iron stand has arrived dismantled, assemble the two legs, joining them with the crosspiece or crosspieces provided. Tightening the knobs is enough to obtain a rigid assembly. Fix the wooden shelf to the back of the machine and the box to the left leg.

Join the connecting rod to the carriage with the screw p, and screw on the crank handle J (fig. 1).

Slide the yarn carriers onto their bar, yarn carrier No. 1 on the front, yarn carrier No. 2 on the rear (see F and B, fig. 2).

Screw onto the wooden shelf the socket B, fig. 10, through which passes the column of the tension mast, which is clamped in this socket. On the column place the crosspiece that receives the tension units (see A, fig. 10) and screw these to the crosspiece.

If the machine was ordered without the cast-iron stand, choose for it a perfectly level table with an absolutely flat surface, or the machine will work badly.

Fix the two clamping brackets to the machine with the screws that held it in the crate; set them against the edge of the table and screw them firmly, using either bolts or large wood screws.

Take great care that the machine is horizontal and that both brackets lie perfectly against the table.

If this is not the case, small wedges of wood or cardboard must be inserted between the table and the brackets at the points required.

Then fix the tension mast; for this, fix the socket B, fig. 10, to the table with wood screws.

Cleaning and greasing.

The needle beds, bars and cam boxes must be cleaned with rags of the anti-rust petroleum jelly that covers them.

The only really effective way of cleaning the needles is to take them out of the needle bed and rub them with a brush or a rag soaked in petroleum benzine.

10

Likewise clean the grooves with a brush and petroleum benzine, and put the needles back in place, taking care not to damage the latches.

Then lightly oil the rubbing parts.

Preparing the cones.

The yarn must be wound on cones in such a way that it can unwind evenly, without any resistance. The swift and the cone winder are fixed at some distance from each other on the edge of a table (see fig. 11).

Place the skein on the swift, fit the cone firmly on the spindle of the winder and wind the yarn onto the cone, starting from the bottom and moving gradually towards the tip, to obtain a true cone.

Fig. 12 shows a well-made cone, fig. 13 a faulty one.

While winding, move the yarn to and fro so that the turns cross one another. If this precaution is neglected, the turns lie parallel on top of one another and, if the skein stops suddenly, the yarn is pulled tight and sinks into the wound part. Unwinding is then faulty.

If the skein has knots, do not let them pass but replace them with the weaver’s knot or the reef knot, see fig. 14 and 15. Above all, never wax the knots.

Wax the yarn, especially wool of inferior or harsh quality, by passing it as it is wound over a piece of special Dubied paraffin wax held in the left hand guiding the yarn (see fig. 11).

Threading the yarn.

From the cone the yarn rises vertically to an eyelet on the tension mast crosspiece (fig. 16), passes from there to the eyelet a, enters the tension discs b between the two washers, which are parted to bring it into the notch of the spindle, goes through the eyelet c of the tension arm R and finally the eyelet d, from which it goes directly to the yarn-carrier tip, which brings it to the needles.

The tension is set as follows with the tension discs b and the drum T:

turn the drum T so as to bring the tension arm R roughly upright, slackening the yarn so that it does not act on the arm,

take hold of the yarn below the eyelet d and pull it evenly. When the eyelet c of the arm comes roughly level with the tension discs b, the yarn should begin to unwind from the cone. If the yarn unwinds sooner, tighten the tension discs a little; if on the contrary it does not unwind easily, the tension is too strong, and the tension discs must be loosened.

As a general rule, try to keep the tension to a minimum, so as to avoid any needless strain on the yarn. However, the coarser the gauge, the thicker the yarns used and the stronger the tension must be.

Using the Edge-Weight Loop.

When knitting a piece of some length, there comes a moment when the weights hanging from the comb touch the floor. They must then be raised to maintain the pull on the knitting. This can be done in two ways:

  1. Roll the knitting round the comb by turning it, so that it rises towards the needle beds, and place the hooks of the weight holders on the back of the comb.

    This method is suitable if the length of the knitted piece does not exceed one metre; beyond that, the roll of knitting round the comb becomes too bulky and awkward.

  2. Take off the weights and the comb and use the edge-weight loop, as follows:
    1. Pass the knitting down through the loop, so as to have the 2 tubes in front (fig. 20).
    2. Pass the knitting up between the two tubes and let it fall in front of the first tube (fig. 21).
    3. Place the edge-weight loop as high as possible, horizontally, tip the weight bar over and hang the weights on it (see fig. 1).

To raise the edge-weight loop when the weights reach the floor, take hold of the selvedges of the knitting in front of the edge-weight loop and pull the knitting so as to slide the edge-weight loop towards the needle beds.

Accidents during work. How to put them right.

This chapter is to be consulted whenever a mishap occurs during work.

1° Damaged needles.

Needle butts can be bent by the carriage:

  1. if needles in the out-of-work position are not pushed down far enough,
  2. if needles in the working position are not pushed up far enough,
  3. if the needles have been put in work otherwise than by their springs,

Needles must never be put in work otherwise than by pushing them by their springs.

  1. if safety springs are broken,
  2. if pins 1 to 4 (fig. 2) are not fully in or fully out.

Needle hooks can be damaged:

  1. by the passage of a large knot,
  2. during the cast-on, if they catch on the steel wire of the comb.

Needle latches can be bent:

if the yarn-carrier tip is set too low and rubs on the needles,

if the brushes are badly set.

To replace a faulty needle,

take off the weights, remove the needle-retaining strip with the hook supplied for this purpose with the machine, and replace the needle in question with a new one, taking care to place the new needle high enough for the strip not to meet its latch. Put the stitch on the needle, replace the strip and the weights, and continue working.

To replace a safety spring,

unscrew the fixing screw of the spring cover strip nearest the faulty spring, then slip a screwdriver under the strip to lift it slightly. Take out the spring and insert a new one from below.

2° Broken yarn.

The yarn breaks (especially with yarns of inferior quality) in the following cases:

  1. if it is badly wound (unevenly wound on the cone) or badly waxed,
  2. if it is too tight or wrongly threaded through the tension unit,
  3. if it has knots that are too large or badly made.

Join the two ends of the yarn with the weaver’s knot or the reef knot (see fig. 14 and 15).

If a hole has appeared in the knitting, pick up the dropped stitches and, if necessary, undo a few rows (see page 19).

3° Badly formed stitches.

Stitches form badly or not at all:

  1. if the knitting is too loose (knobs set too low) or too tight (knobs set too high),
  2. if the yarn-carrier tip is badly set (too high or too low),
  3. if the yarn carrier is twisted or bent and does not bring the yarn to the right place,
  4. if the knitting is very wide and too little weight has been hung; the stitches are then faulty at the selvedges.

    This fault is seen especially in tubular knitting; in this case one can also hook on to the selvedges the claws and small weights supplied with the machine.

  5. if the latch of a needle opens with difficulty; the small groove of the needle must then be cleaned with a pin, or the needle replaced.

4° Stitches drop,

  1. if one knits too fast, especially in full or half cardigan,
  2. if the pull on the knitting is insufficient (add weights),
  3. if the needles lack oil and jump in their grooves; this is what is called needle jumping.

5° The needles lift the knitting,

  1. if the latch is bent; it can be straightened with pliers, or the needle changed,
  2. if the knitting is too tight (knobs set too high),
  3. if the weights are insufficient (see table page 14),
  4. if one knits a harsh material without having waxed it.

6° The carriage jams,

  1. if the butt of a needle out of work is not fully lowered,
  2. if the carriage is brought back before the end of its pass, that is, before it has fully left the field of needles in work.

To free the carriage, find out which needle is jamming it by trying to move all the engaged needles one after another. For this operation it is better to remove the brushes, so as not to damage them.

Pull the jammed needle upwards or push it, while trying at the same time to move the carriage.

Sometimes, to free the carriage, there is nothing for it but to unscrew the guide bars and lift it. In that case, take care afterwards to put them back exactly in place.

If the carriage jams while working with the Super-8, see page 38.

7° The knitting is uneven,

  1. if the knobs are not set at their exact position; for this setting to be correct, the eye must not look at an angle but be directly opposite the pointer c, fig. 7,
  2. if the front bed is not in its normal position: pull or push the racking handle F fully home and pull the bed-lowering handle H, fig. 1;
  3. if the yarn used is uneven (poor quality) or unevenly waxed;
  4. if one knits at an uneven speed.

8° The knitting has vertical stripes,

  1. if needles run stiffly, either because their butt has been bent by a knock or because their groove is clogged;
  2. if needles are damaged (bent or stiff latch).

9° The selvedges are faulty,

  1. if the tension is too weak or too strong;
  2. if the yarn carrier is taken too far from the selvedges;
  3. if the brushes are badly set and catch the yarn (see “Setting the brushes”, fig. 8);
  4. if the amount of weight hanging from the knitting is insufficient;
  5. if the knitting is too tight (knobs set too high);
  6. if the yarn carrier is bent, that is, twisted to the left or to the right. In the 1st case the selvedge will be faulty on the right; in the 2nd case it will be faulty on the left.

The setting of the yarn carrier is checked as follows:

Remove the rear brush.

Work the front needles only, and run the carriage with the yarn carrier without yarn.

Observe the position of the needle directly below the yarn-carrier tip: the tip of the latch must project at least 1 mm beyond the lower end of the yarn-carrier tip.

Make this check by stopping the carriage once in its pass from right to left, then once in its pass from left to right.

How to undo a few rows of knitting on the machine and how to pick up a dropped stitch.

Holes may sometimes appear in the knitting. If it is simply a dropped stitch, it can be picked up once the article is finished, with a machine needle, in the same way as for the chain cast-off. But as soon as a dropped stitch is noticed during work, take the precaution of securing it so that it runs no further; it is enough to pass a needle through the stitch to hold it.

If the damage is more serious and there is a real hole, caused for example by a large knot passing or by the yarn breaking, it is better to re-stitch the needles at the place where the hole occurred and then undo a few rows of knitting. The repair made in this way will be invisible, which would not be so if it were done afterwards.

To undo a few rows, first take off the weights hanging from the comb, then proceed as follows:

Take hold of the end of the yarn between the yarn-carrier tip and the needles and pull it gently in the direction in which the knitting comes undone, so as to raise the needles a little. At the same time the stitches of the last row but one will be brought one after another to the level of the needle heads (hook), and small jerks given to the yarn will pass these stitches into the hooks of the corresponding needles. With a little practice this can be done very quickly.

In the case of full cardigan knitting, before undoing, lower the needles in the half-drop position so as to form the stitches.

Using the transfer tool.

To obtain directly on the machine the proper shape of certain articles or parts of articles, increases or decreases are made, using the working hook and the decreasing transfer tool.

The use of the latter is shown in detail in fig. 33, 34 and 35.

Hold the tool in the right hand and, with the left hand, push up by their butts the needles on which the transfer is made, so as to open the latches and free the hooks.

Fig. 33. Catch the needle by its hook with the tool and pull it up until the stitch is behind the latch.

Fig. 34. Bring the needle down, pushing it with the tool, until the stitch has slid onto the tool. Free the tool from the needle hook without letting go of the stitch.

Tip the tool so that the stitch drops into the needle hook.

The transfer tool is used for all kinds of work: decreases, stitch holding, openwork and plated patterns, chain cast-off, pleats, etc.

Increases can be made by either of the following two methods:

First method. Put one more needle in work, on the front and on the rear in turn, on the side where the carriage is.

For decreases, the two- or three-point transfer tools are preferably used, so that the double stitches resulting from the transfer lie a little inside the selvedge.

with the left hand, raise the 3 or 4 selvedge needles a little to open their latches,

catch the 2 or 3 selvedge needles with the tool, pull them up and then bring them down so as to pass the stitches onto the tool,

transfer the stitches one needle further on, towards the middle of the knitting, pass them into the needle hooks and lower by its butt the needle that has become free.

Plating.

So-called “plated” knitting is made of two yarns of different kinds, for example a silk yarn and a cotton yarn, or a wool yarn and a cotton yarn, or else two yarns of different colours.

These two yarns are knitted simultaneously in such a way that one covers the other.

In tubular knitting the first will be visible on the right side, while the other will be visible on the wrong side.

In ribbed knitting (1 × 1, full cardigan, etc.) one of the yarns will be visible on both faces of the knitting, while the other will be hidden inside.

To obtain plated knitting, a special yarn-carrier tip called a plating tip is used, which simply replaces the ordinary tip (see fig. 37).

The plating tip is used for example for knitting articles in half-wool (wool m, cotton n, fig. 37), or to reinforce the toe and heel of stockings and socks (the reinforcing yarn is passed through the half-moon opening), or else to obtain colour effects (transfer patterns).

Plating is also useful when an article is knitted from two different materials of which only one is to be visible, such as a silk and cotton article meant to look like pure silk, or ladies’ winter stockings in wool and silk, which are to look like silk stockings while being warmer than ordinary silk stockings.

The yarn that is to be visible on the knitting (yarn m) is passed through the central hole a of the tip; the other (yarn n), which is to be hidden, is passed through the half-moon opening. Each of the two yarns must have its own tension, so two tension units are needed for each plating tip.

To obtain good plating, the following precautions must be taken:

  1. check that the tip is exactly on the centre line of the machine, 1 mm. above the needle latches (see fig. 38);
  2. give more tension to yarn n, in the half-moon entry, than to yarn m;
  3. wax the materials carefully so that they do not risk tangling;
  4. hang more weight from the comb than for ordinary knitting.

The plating tip is not included in the normal accessories; it is supplied on request, as is the additional tension unit.

This tip, generally little known, is of great service and we recommend it to every knitter, for it allows her to increase at little cost the variety of articles produced on the machine.

THE SUPER-8.

The Dubied SUPER-8 is a new patented device that can be fitted to all Dubied hand machines of types MM, MR and CS in gauges 25, 28, 32 and 36. It makes it possible to produce on these very simple machines, without special training, patterned knitting with the same appearance as Jacquard knitting.

The Super-8.

The SUPER-8 consists of a support or frame in which the body D, fig. 42, pivots. The device shown in this figure is in its “in work” position, since the body D is engaged in the hook C. To put the device in the “out of work” position, the hook C is pushed to the left and the body D is lifted by two springs.

The essential part of the SUPER-8 is the drum G, bearing the selector cams I. The lever H is used to turn the drum by hand; just pull it towards you, then turn it in one direction or the other so as to bring the required number to the top of the drum. In fig. 42, the drum is in position 1.

The selector cams are small rotary cams of extremely precise manufacture. Their edge, cut to suit the needle selection to be obtained, acts on the needle butts and gives them an impulse either towards the top or towards the bottom of the bed.

To knit checks or vertical stripes, remove the two selector cams in positions 5 and 6 and replace them with the two inverted selector cams of set 0. (See pages 39 and 40: “Changing selector cams.”)

In addition to these two sets, there are several further sets of 8 selector cams. Each of these sets gives new series of patterns, so that knitters have every interest in owning several

Cut articles.

The method of knitting pieces quite straight and then cutting them to the required shape is applied mainly by hosiery manufacturers; it is, however, beginning to spread among home knitters, especially for SUPER-8 articles and for transfer patterns. The loss of material is then small compared with the time saved by knitting without increases or decreases.

Care and running of the machine.

To look after the machine, remove the dust from the guide bars and the carriage every evening with a rag and a brush, then every morning put a little oil on the guide bars of the yarn carriers and the carriage.

Do not forget to grease from time to time the sloping faces of the ring that drives the counter, the supports of the crank axle and the parts of the stop blocks in contact with the driving plunger of the yarn carriers.

Regularly and carefully remove the build-ups of material that form after some time at work in the entries of the bed blocks, on the lowering brackets, under the tilting cams, etc.

Take special care that no drop of oil ever falls on the needle field.

Always use the special DUBIED oil.

Racking attachment for the Super-8.

This attachment, which can be fitted to all Dubied machines with the Super-8, is fixed to the left-hand wall of the frame. It shifts the starting position of the Super-8 selector cams, thus giving original effects such as zigzags, chevrons and staggered motifs.

The essential part of the attachment is a drum A, milled with stepped grooves. There are 9 of these grooves in gauges 32 and 36 and 12 in gauges 25 and 28. The spacing of 2 grooves for each gauge is the same as that of the needles on the bed.

With a lever B, the drum can be turned so as to bring a greater or smaller number of grooves into line with the bed, at the point where the toothed pinion (small toothed wheel) of the Super-8 selector cam in work engages.

The Super-8 racking attachment can be put in action only when the carriage is fully to the left.

Using the attachment.

Before starting to knit, check that the drum A, fig. 65, is in the out-of-work position. This position is recognised by the fact that no groove of the drum A lies in the path of the small toothed wheels of the Super-8. To obtain this out-of-work position, one need only pull the lever of the attachment towards oneself until the drum locks.

Begin knitting and put the Super-8 in action in the usual way.

The indicator strips for racked Super-8 patterns bear figures appearing in window 3, showing the position of the drum of the racking attachment. As the pattern was begun with the drum at position 0, the racking attachment is worked only when a figure appears in window 3. If this figure is for example 5, the lever of the racking attachment must be pressed fully 5 times. At each press the drum makes a fraction of a turn, which brings one more groove into the path of the toothed wheel of the selector cam.

Continue in the same way, taking care always to work the lever of the racking attachment fully home.

To go from positions 0 to 9, press the lever; to go from positions 9 to 0, pull the lever.
